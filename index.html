The caseWhy the Fraser Valley needs Valley Sentinel

Most new human diseases do not start in people. The World Organisation for Animal Health estimates that more than 75% of emerging infectious diseases in humans are zoonotic, meaning they spread from animals (World Organisation for Animal Health, 2024). The World Health Organization promotes a "One Health" approach, which treats the health of people, animals and the environment as one connected system instead of separate fields (World Health Organization, 2023). If the next pandemic is likely to begin in animals, the best place to stop it is where people and animals meet most closely.

In British Columbia, that place is the Fraser Valley. The valley is home to about 80% of the province's poultry farms (CBC News, 2022), and Fraser Valley East and Abbotsford together earned 37% of all farm revenue in BC in 2018 (Statistics Canada, 2021). The same region has 324,005 residents (Statistics Canada, 2022a) and is one of the fastest-growing regional districts in the province (Fraser Valley Regional District, 2024). Dense farms, a growing population and a busy border crossing make the valley a front line for animal-to-human disease.

This is not a theory. In 2004, an H7N3 avian influenza outbreak led Canadian officials to order the depopulation of all poultry south of the Fraser River, about 19 million birds (CIDRAP, 2004). In 2014, an H5N2 outbreak on farms in Abbotsford and Chilliwack was the first time a Eurasian H5 bird flu virus caused an outbreak in domestic poultry in North America (Canadian Food Inspection Agency, 2015). In the 2023 season, 52 BC farms, mostly in the Fraser Valley, were infected with highly pathogenic H5N1 (BC Ministry of Agriculture and Food, 2023a).

The virus has already crossed into people here. In November 2024, a teenager in the Fraser Health region became the first person known to catch H5N1 bird flu inside Canada, and was in critical condition in hospital (CBC News, 2024). Officials found the virus was closely related to strains circulating in BC poultry, but could not determine how the teen was infected. Just across the border, Washington State reported nine human H5 cases among poultry workers in 2024 (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2024), and in October 2026 H5N1 was confirmed in Washington dairy cattle for the first time (Washington State Department of Agriculture, 2026). Each human infection gives the virus another chance to adapt to spread between people.

We already know what an unprepared pandemic costs. The Auditor General of Canada found that the Public Health Agency of Canada was not as well prepared for COVID-19 as it could have been (Auditor General of Canada, 2021a), and that long-known problems with the national stockpile had not been fixed (Auditor General of Canada, 2021b). At least 37,560 Canadians died directly from COVID-19 between January 2020 and June 2022 (Statistics Canada, 2022b), more than 32,400 British Columbians had surgery postponed or never scheduled in the first wave (CBC News, 2021), and federal emergency aid cost about $169 billion in 2020 alone (CBC News, 2020).

Current funding is far too small for the risk. BC created a $5 million Farmed Animal Disease Program in 2023 to help farmers prepare for outbreaks (BC Ministry of Agriculture and Food, 2023b), and a $2.5 million program is helping about 75 Fraser Valley poultry farms install air filtration and UV equipment (BC Ministry of Agriculture and Food, 2025a). These programs work, but they reach only a fraction of farms. The province's Animal Health Centre in Abbotsford is a key diagnostic lab for avian influenza and handles more than 10,000 cases a year (BC Ministry of Agriculture and Food, 2025b), but animal labs and human health labs still work largely apart.

Our idea in one sentence: Valley Sentinel links farm biosecurity, constant environmental testing, a shared animal-and-human lab at UFV, protection for farm workers and a rapid response unit into one system, so a dangerous virus is caught in a barn or a wetland and stopped before it becomes the next pandemic.

Item 2Scope

Scope management defines and controls what the project does and does not include (Project Management Institute [PMI], 2021). A firm scope keeps a $1B program from drifting into everything related to agriculture.

In scope

  • Valley One Health Lab at UFV's Chilliwack campus, which already hosts agriculture and health sciences programs (University of the Fraser Valley, n.d.). It tests animal, environmental and human samples side by side and sequences virus genomes.
  • Farm Biosecurity Upgrade Fund: grants for air filtration, UV systems, controlled-entry rooms, wild-bird-proofing and wash stations on up to 1,000 poultry, dairy and hog farms.
  • Sentinel Surveillance Network: sampling at wetlands used by migrating wild birds, wastewater testing at the JAMES plant in Abbotsford, which also serves Mission and Sumas, Washington (City of Abbotsford, n.d.), bulk-milk testing on dairy farms and air sensors in poultry barns.
  • Farm Worker Health Program: free protective equipment, seasonal flu shots, rapid testing and mobile clinics, with outreach in workers' own languages.
  • Rapid Response Unit: trained teams and equipment to contain an infected farm within 24 hours, plus fast compensation so farmers report early.
  • One Health Data Platform: one secure dashboard shared by CFIA, the provincial Animal Health Centre, Fraser Health and the BC Centre for Disease Control.

Out of scope

  • Changing CFIA's legal authority or federal compensation rules.
  • Vaccine development or manufacturing for animals or people.
  • Replacing the provincial Animal Health Centre, which keeps its own role.
  • Operating costs after December 31, 2029, beyond the sustainment fund.

The biggest stakeholder risk is that farmers fear a positive test means losing their flock and income. We handle this by putting farmers on the advisory board, keeping farm data confidential except where reporting is legally required, and paying compensation advances quickly through the Rapid Response Unit. Disagreements go to the steering committee within two weeks.

Item 7Champion

A champion is a senior leader who promotes the project and helps it get resources and support (Schwalbe, 2019). Valley Sentinel has two champions. The executive champion is the President of UFV, who can commit campus land at Chilliwack, link the agriculture and health sciences faculties, and argue the case to government. The industry champion is the chair of the BC Poultry Association, because farmer trust is the program's biggest dependency. A respected voice from the industry telling other farmers to join is worth more than any advertising.

Item 6Four frames of organizations: UFV

Bolman and Deal (2017) describe four frames for understanding organizations.

  • Structural frame: UFV makes decisions through its Board of Governors, Senate and faculties. The project crosses two faculties (agriculture and health sciences), so we create one joint "One Health" steering group with clear authority to avoid gaps between departments.
  • Human resources frame: Faculty want research and teaching opportunities, not extra unpaid work. The project funds new positions, graduate research and new programs, so staff see it as growth.
  • Political frame: Farmers, government agencies and the university all want influence. A shared governance model with seats for each group, and fair placement of jobs across Chilliwack and Abbotsford, reduces conflict.
  • Symbolic frame: UFV presents itself as the university of the Fraser Valley, rooted in its farming communities and on Stó:lō territory. "Protecting the valley's farms and families" fits that identity and helps win support.

Item 9Project life cycle model

Valley Sentinel uses a hybrid life cycle. The lab building follows a waterfall (predictive) model, because construction and biosafety certification have to happen in a fixed order with approved designs. The surveillance network and the data platform follow a spiral model, in which each loop sets goals, analyzes risks, builds and tests a prototype, then plans the next loop (Boehm, 1988). This suits them because we will only learn which sensors and data views work best by testing them on real farms. Farm upgrades are delivered in three waves, so lessons from wave 1 improve waves 2 and 3. Schwalbe (2019) notes that the life cycle should match how certain the requirements are.

What the finished system does: barn to response

This second flow chart shows how Valley Sentinel works once running. Each step comes from a project deliverable.

Signal

A barn air sensor, milk sample or wetland swab tests positive for H5.

Confirm

One Health Lab confirms and sequences the virus; checks for signs it is adapting to mammals.

Alert

Data platform notifies CFIA, the Ministry, Fraser Health and nearby farms.

Lock down

Farms in the zone switch to high-biosecurity mode using their upgraded entry rooms.

Protect people

Mobile clinic tests and protects exposed workers and families.

Contain

Rapid Response Unit supports CFIA to contain the farm; compensation advance is paid.

Item 11Pre-initiation tasks

Senior managers should complete key groundwork before a project officially begins (Schwalbe, 2019). In December 2026 we will:

  • Confirm funding terms and reporting requirements with the federal sponsor.
  • Hire the Program Director and form the steering committee.
  • Meet CFIA, the Ministry, Fraser Health, farm associations and First Nations to agree on roles.
  • Review lessons from the 2004, 2014 and 2022–2024 outbreaks and the Auditor General's pandemic audits.
  • Confirm a lab site at UFV Chilliwack and start biosafety and zoning checks.
  • Split the program into six workstreams, each with a manager and budget.

Weaknesses

Items 15 & 17Quality management, testing and cost of quality

Quality management makes sure the project satisfies the needs it was started for, through planning, assurance and control (PMI, 2021). Quality standards for Valley Sentinel include the Canadian Biosafety Standard for the lab, ISO/IEC 17025 accreditation for testing competence, CFIA-approved diagnostic methods, and the national biosecurity standards for poultry and dairy farms.

Testing plan

  • Proficiency testing: the lab tests blind samples from reference labs and must match their results before it reports real ones.
  • Sensor pilot: 10 farm and wetland sites are tested for six months; sensors must agree with lab results at least 95% of the time.
  • Software testing: unit, integration, security and user acceptance testing of the data platform with CFIA, Ministry and Fraser Health staff.
  • Farm audits: every upgraded farm is inspected before the final grant payment.
  • Exercise Flyway (September 2029): a full-scale simulation in which a fake H5 signal from a wetland moves through every step of the barn-to-response chain. The project cannot close until it passes.

Cost of quality

Cost of quality is the cost of conformance plus the cost of non-conformance. Crosby (1979) argued that doing things right the first time costs less than fixing failures.

Items 18 & 19Human resource management and motivation

The core team grows from 10 people in 2027 to about 180 staff and contractors at peak, not counting construction trades. New hires include:

  • Program Director (PMP), six workstream managers and a program office (scheduler, cost analyst, risk manager).
  • Lab director, virologists, lab technologists, bioinformaticians and a biosafety officer.
  • Veterinarians and animal health technicians for surveillance and response.
  • Biosecurity advisors and inspectors for farm upgrades.
  • Nurses and multilingual outreach workers for the farm worker program.
  • Software developers, a data architect and a privacy officer.
  • An Indigenous engagement lead and a farmer liaison.

Hiring will be hard. Health care job vacancies in Canada reached a record 95,800 in late 2022 (Statistics Canada, 2023), and health workers reported burnout during COVID-19 (Canadian Institute for Health Information, 2023). To avoid taking staff from hospitals, the project trains new lab and animal health technicians through UFV programs funded by the lab budget.

How we will motivate people

Herzberg (1968) found that hygiene factors such as pay, safety and working conditions prevent dissatisfaction, while motivators such as achievement, recognition, responsibility and growth create real motivation. We cover hygiene factors with fair pay, safe working conditions and proper protective equipment. For motivators, we recognize teams at every milestone, give workstreams real decision-making power, pay for professional certifications, and connect the work to its purpose: keeping local farms running and local families safe. Schwalbe (2019) adds that managers should learn what each person values, so managers will hold regular one-on-ones.

Item 25Closing process

Closing formally ends the project, confirms all work is accepted and records lessons learned (PMI, 2021). Valley Sentinel closes from October to December 31, 2029:

  • Get signed acceptance for each deliverable from its operating owner.
  • Hand over the lab to UFV, the farm fund to the Ministry, response equipment to CFIA and the Ministry, and the worker program to Fraser Health.
  • Close all contracts and grants, settle payments and move unused contingency into the sustainment fund.
  • Release project staff with recognition and help moving into permanent roles.
  • Run lessons-learned sessions and publish a final report other farm regions can use.
  • Celebrate go-live with farmers, workers and the community on December 31, 2029.
Valley Sentinel succeeds if the next dangerous virus is found in a barn air filter or a wetland sample, not in a hospital, and is stopped there.
